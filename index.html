Šiqʼāl

The speech of the Šiqʼar
A constructed language — grammar, lexicon, and texts

Šiqʼāl [ʃiˈqʼaːl] is the language of the Šiqʼar, a fictional mountain people of the highland river valleys. It is an isolate: no proven relatives, no written history before the present century, and a rich oral literature of proverbs, laments, riddles, and legal formula. This document is a descriptive grammar of the standard literary variety, together with a core lexicon and five annotated texts.

The design is naturalistic rather than schematic. The sound system borrows the ejective–aspirate–plain stop contrast, the uvulars, and the dense consonant clusters of the Northwest Caucasus; the morphosyntax owes its ergativity and polypersonal agreement to Mayan and Basque; the evidential system is modelled on Tibetan and Quechua; and the initial consonant alternations will look familiar to anyone who has studied the Celtic languages. None of this is genealogy — it is typological taste.

The language at a glance:

A note on the spelling: ʼ is a glottal stop (or the mark of an ejective), ʰ marks aspiration, a macron marks a long vowel, an ogonek marks a nasal vowel, š is sh, ŋ is ng, and c is ts. Stress is marked here only where it is not predictable from the rules in §1.5.

The vowel system is symmetrical: five qualities, each with a short, a long, and a nasal form.

Long vowels are written with a macron (ā ē ī ō ū), nasal vowels with an ogonek (ą ę į ǫ ų). Long nasal vowels are phonologically possible but occur only in a handful of interjections and in the call hǭ, used to drive cattle.

Nasal vowels are strongly nasalised and frequently nasalise a preceding glide or liquid: mąk "hand" is [mɑ̃k], pʼų "five" is [pʼũ]. In the speech of younger speakers, nasal vowels are increasingly replaced by vowel + n, but the literary standard preserves them.

1.3 Allophony

The following processes are regular and automatic.

  1. Intervocalic voicing. Plain stops voice between vowels and after nasals: /p t k q/ → [b d g ɢ]. Thus pāta "master" is [ˈpʰaːda], mukʰa "fish" is [ˈmukʰa], tʼara "stone" is [ˈtʼara] (the ejective does not voice).
  2. Aspiration. Aspirated stops are strongly aspirated word-initially and before a stressed vowel, and weakly aspirated elsewhere. They never occur word-finally.
  3. Nasal assimilation. /n/ assimilates in place to a following consonant: [ŋ] before velars and uvulars, [m] before labials. In rapid speech, where the prefix vowel of na- may be elided, nagāla is [ŋˈgaːla] and naper is [mˈper].
  4. Uvularisation of /x/. /x/ is [χ] adjacent to /q/ and next to /o u/, and [ç] before /i/: xūl [χuːl], xar [xar].
  5. Dark /l/. /l/ is velarised [ɫ] in codas: qʰal [qʰaɫ], but kāla [ˈkʰaːla].
  6. Laxing. Short vowels in unstressed closed syllables are lax: /a/ → [ɐ], /e/ → [ɛ], /o/ → [ɔ]. Long vowels keep their quality in all positions.
  7. Glottal spreading. A glottal stop or an ejective immediately before a vowel gives that vowel a creaky onset; this is most audible in careful speech and in laments.

1.4 Phonotactics

The syllable template is (C)(C)V(C).

  • Onsets. Any single consonant may begin a syllable. Two-consonant onsets are limited to obstruent + l r y w (pr, kl, qʼy, xw) and s + stop (sp, st, sk, sq). No other initial clusters occur.
  • Codas. Any consonant except h and the aspirated stops may close a syllable. Word-finally the same set occurs, but x is avoided.
  • No geminates. Identical consonants simplify across morpheme boundaries: š-šiqʼa → šiqʼa.
  • Hiatus. Sequences of vowels across morpheme boundaries are resolved by glide insertion (y after front vowels, w after back vowels) or by contraction.

Some representative words: tʼerka [ˈtʼer.ka] "wagon", šiqʼāl [ʃiˈqʼaːl] "language", kālya [ˈkʰaːl.ja] "village", pʼenya [ˈpʼen.ja] "wool", qʼāla [ˈqʼaː.la] "sky".

1.5 Stress

Stress is weight-sensitive and falls on the rightmost heavy syllable of the word. A syllable is heavy if it contains a long vowel, a nasal vowel, or a coda consonant. If no syllable is heavy, stress falls on the first syllable.

1.6 Initial lenition

Šiqʼāl has a system of initial consonant alternations — lenition — which is one of the language's most characteristic features. It is triggered by three constructions: possessive prefixes, the prepositions a "to" and e "from" (and xam "with"), and the negative prefix u-.

The triggers are consistent:

  • Possessive prefixes: kāla "house" → nagāla "my house"; pʰer "skin" → naper "my skin"; qʰara "mountain" → naqara "my mountain".
  • Prepositions: a kāla → a gāla "to the house"; e qʰara → e qara "from the mountain"; xam tʰer → xam ter "with the man".
  • Negation: kʰen "say" → u-ken "not say"; pʰan "work" → u-pan "not work"; kʼal "build" → u-kʼal (ejectives do not lenite).

Lenition is written at morpheme boundaries but not inside roots. That is why tʰapa "bread" is spelled with p even though the p is pronounced [b] between vowels, while nabāta is spelled with b. The orthography is morphophonemic: it writes the alternation where it carries grammar.

2. Writing system

Šiqʼāl is written in an abugida called Sīrāl ("writing"), a relatively recent development in the language's history. The script is written in vertical columns running top to bottom, with columns ordered left to right — the opposite of the neighbouring valley scripts, a fact the Šiqʼar are fond of mentioning.

Each consonant has a base glyph and carries an inherent vowel a. Vowel signs are attached to the right side of the base glyph (which, in vertical orientation, means below it). The system distinguishes:

  • short vowels by a single stroke or dot;
  • long vowels by a doubled stroke;
  • nasal vowels by a small circle above the base;
  • ejectives by a spur or notch on the base glyph;
  • aspirates by a doubling of the base's left edge.

Word-initial vowels use a silent carrier glyph. There is no case distinction, no punctuation beyond a vertical bar for clause boundaries and a double bar for paragraph breaks, and no spaces between words in traditional orthography — a practice that makes legal documents notoriously difficult for children.

The romanization used in this document is the standard scholarly transliteration, not the native script. A full palaeography of Sīrāl is beyond the scope of this grammar.

3. Nouns

Nouns are not marked for gender or noun class. They inflect for number and case, and they take possessive prefixes when the possessor is pronominal. Definiteness is not grammatical: kāla can mean "house", "a house", or "the house" according to context.

3.1 Number

The plural is formed with a suffix.

  • After a vowel: -r.
  • After a consonant: -Vr, where the vowel harmonises with the last vowel of the stem: -ir after i, e; -ur after o, u; -ar after a.

Before a root that begins with a vowel, the prefix vowel is deleted: na- + axa "father" → naxa "my father"; ta- + ama "mother" → tama "your mother"; ša- + ʼasa "fire" → šaʼasa "his fire" (the glottal stop blocks deletion).

The prefixes combine with case suffixes in the expected order: nagālaš "my house (ERG)", nagālan "of my house", nagālakha "to my house".

Full-noun possession uses the genitive, with the possessed noun first (head-initial): kāla tʰerin "the man's house", nāma nan "my name" (literally "name of-me").

3.4 Demonstratives

There are two demonstratives, sa "this" and su "that". They are invariant and precede the noun: sa kāla "this house", su kʼur "that horse". With a case-marked noun the demonstrative stays put: sa kālami "in this house".

From them are formed the locational adverbs sami "here" and sumi "there" (literally "at this" and "at that").

Independent pronouns distinguish first, second, and third person, singular and plural, and — in the third person — human and non-human. They take the same case suffixes as nouns.

4. Adjectives and comparison

Šiqʼāl has a small closed class of true adjectives, most of them borrowings or relics. The great majority of property concepts — "big", "cold", "good", "heavy" — are stative verbs, which inflect like any other verb when predicative.

When a stative verb modifies a noun, it takes the attributive participle suffix -i and follows the noun. The suffix replaces a final -a of the stem (kʼatʼa → kʼatʼi) and surfaces as -y after other vowels (pʰā → pʰāy):

  • kāla pʰali "a big house" (pʰal "be big")
  • tʰer kʼatʼi "a strong man" (kʼatʼa "be strong")
  • pʼenya pʰāy "warm wool" (pʰā "be warm")
  • kālar pʰalir "big houses" — the participle agrees in number with the noun.

Predicatively, the stative verb is the main verb of the clause and precedes its subject:

  • Pʰali kāla. "The house is big."
  • Kʼatʼay tʰer. "The man is strong."
  • Pʰāy pʼenya. "The wool is warm."

The participle -i is also the source of a large number of lexical adjectives: sāmi "good", kʼari "bad", tūmi "quick".

Adverbs are formed from stative verbs with -an, with contraction after a vowel: tūma "be quick" → tūman "quickly"; sāma "be good" → sāman "well".

Comparison uses the adverb xān "more" and the ablative of the standard:

  • Xān pʰali wa tʼaratar. "It is bigger than a stone."
  • Xān kʼatʼay sa natar? "Is he stronger than me?"

There is no dedicated superlative; "the biggest" is expressed with xān and a partitive phrase, or with qʼama "all": xān pʰali wa qʼamartar "it is bigger than all animals".

5. Numerals

Šiqʼāl counts in twelves. The numerals one to twelve are roots; thirteen to twenty-three are additive compounds with cʼa "twelve"; multiples of twelve are multiplicative; and 144 has its own root.

Numerals precede the noun. From two to twelve the noun stays singular — a relic of an older system in which the numeral was a noun and the counted noun its apposition:

  • so kʼur "one horse"
  • tʼi kʼur "two horses"
  • cʼa kʼur "twelve horses"
  • cʼa-so kʼur "thirteen horses"
  • tʼi cʼa kʼur "twenty-four horses"
  • cʼāya kʼur "144 horses"

Ordinals are formed with -ti: soti "first", tʼiti "second", kʰalti "third". They precede the noun like cardinals: soti kʼur "the first horse".

The interrogative tʼar "how many" behaves like a numeral: Tʼar kʼur? "How many horses?"

The duodecimal base is reflected in the culture: bread is sold by the dozen, land is measured in twelfths, and the traditional year is divided into twelve months named after the moons.

6. Verbs

The verb is the centre of the Šiqʼāl clause. It carries aspect, evidentiality, and agreement with both the ergative and the absolutive argument, and it is the host of a rich system of valency-changing derivation.

6.1 The verb template

The maximal verb has the following structure:

ContentNEGROOTDERIVASPECTEVIDERGABS
Formu-—-tʼi, -na, -ma, -pʼi, -ya, -ul, -kʰu-Ø, -wa, -tʼu, -lo-i, -yu, -ka, -šu, -Ø-ni, -ti, -ši, -mi, -ci, -ri-n, -t, -Ø, -m, -c, -r

Only the root is obligatory. A minimal verb is a bare root, which is interpreted as perfective, assumed-evidential, third-person singular:

  • Wāy. "He came, as one would expect."

A maximal verb can be long:

  • U-qʼen-tʼi-kʰu-wa-ka-ši-n. "He apparently could not kill me."

In practice, such forms are rare; the example is given to show the template.

6.2 Aspect

Šiqʼāl has no grammatical tense. It has four aspects, and time is located by adverbs and context.

AspectSuffixMeaningExample
Perfective-Øcompleted eventxari "saw"
Imperfective-waongoing, habitualxarwa "sees, is seeing"
Resultative-tʼustate resulting from an eventxartʼu "has seen"
Prospective-loabout to, willxarlo "will see"

The perfective is the narrative default. The imperfective covers progressive and habitual senses; there is no separate habitual. The resultative is stative: xartʼu "has seen" implies that the seeing is relevant now. The prospective marks future or imminent events, often with tʰan "then, later".

  • Xar-i-ši-Ø tʰeriš kāla. "The man saw the house."
  • Xar-wa-ši-Ø tʰeriš kāla. "The man sees the house."
  • Xar-tʼu-ši-Ø tʰeriš kāla. "The man has seen the house."
  • Xar-lo-ši-Ø tʰeriš kāla. "The man will see the house."

6.3 Evidentiality

Every declarative verb carries an evidential suffix, obligatorily. The system distinguishes five values.

EvidentialSuffixMeaning
Visual-iI saw it
Sensory-yuI heard, felt, or otherwise sensed it
Inferential-kaI infer it from evidence
Reported-šuI was told; hearsay
Assumed-ØIt is expected, obvious, or common knowledge

The evidential follows the aspect and precedes agreement. After a vowel, -i becomes -y; after -wa, the sequence contracts to -way.

  • Wāy-i-Ø. → Wāyi. "He came — I saw him."
  • Wāy-yu-Ø. → Wāyyu. "He came — I heard him."
  • Wāy-ka-Ø. → Wāyka. "He must have come — I infer it."
  • Wāy-šu-Ø. → Wāyšu. "He came, they say."
  • Wāy-Ø. → Wāy. "He came, of course."

The choice is pragmatic, not syntactic. A speaker who did not witness an event but has no reason to doubt it may use the assumed form; a speaker reporting a rumour uses -šu; a speaker who saw the wet ground uses -ka. In stories, the visual is the default for the narrative present, while the reported is used for events outside the narrator's experience.

Evidentials are not used in imperatives, optatives, or converbs.

6.5 Mood

The indicative is unmarked. The other moods are:

  • Imperative. Suffix -a (2SG), -aya (2PL). No evidential, no agreement.
  • Yut-a! "Go!"
  • Yut-aya! "Go, all of you!"
  • Qʼan-a! "Listen!"
  • Prohibitive. The negative prefix with the imperative: U-yut-a! "Don't go!"
  • Optative. Suffix -šay: Yut-šay. "May he go."
  • Conditional. The particle kʼu at the head of the clause: Kʼu yut-wa-t, xar-lo-ni-Ø. "If you go, I will see it."

The conditional particle is not a suffix; it is a clitic that precedes the verb. The apodosis is unmarked.

6.6 Valency and derivation

Šiqʼāl has a productive set of valency-changing suffixes. They occupy slot 3, before aspect.

SuffixFunctionExample
-tʼicausativeqʼen "die" → qʼentʼi "kill"
-napassive / anticausativexar "see" → xarna "be seen"
-maantipassivexar "see" → xarma "look, be looking"
-pʼireflexivexar "see" → xarpʼi "see oneself"
-yareciprocalxar "see" → xarya "see each other"
-uldesiderativeyut "go" → yutul "want to go"
-kʰuabilitiveyut "go" → yutkʰu "be able to go"

Causative. The causee becomes the absolutive object; the original agent, if it is expressed, takes the dative.

  • Qʼen-tʼi-i-ši-Ø tʰeriš kʼur. "The man killed the horse." (literally "caused the horse to die")

Passive. The patient becomes the absolutive subject; the agent is omitted or marked with the ablative.

  • Xar-na-i-Ø kāla. "The house was seen."
  • Xar-na-i-Ø kāla tʰer-tar. "The house was seen by the man."

Antipassive. The agent becomes the absolutive subject, and the object — if present — takes the dative. The antipassive is used when the object is indefinite or unimportant, and it is a common way to relativise an agent.

  • Xar-ma-i-Ø tʰer. "The man was looking."
  • Xar-ma-i-Ø tʰer kālakha. "The man was looking at the house."

Reflexive and reciprocal. These detransitivise the verb; the subject is absolutive.

  • Xar-pʼi-i-Ø tʰer. "The man saw himself."
  • Xar-ya-r. "They saw each other."

Desiderative and abilitive.

  • Yut-ul-wa-n. "I want to go."
  • Yut-kʰu-wa-n. "I can go."
  • U-yut-kʰu-wa-n. "I cannot go."

6.7 Negation

Negation is expressed by the prefix u- on the verb. The prefix triggers lenition of a following stop or affricate, and it precedes the root, before all derivational suffixes.

  • U-wāy-i-Ø. "He did not come."
  • U-xar-i-ni-Ø. "I did not see it."
  • U-kʰen-i-ši-Ø. → Ukeniši. "He did not say it."
  • U-pʰan-wa-n. → Upanwan. "I am not working."

Negative existence and possession use the particle uma:

  • Uma kāla. "There is no house."
  • Uma nakha kāla. "I have no house."

The difference between u- and uma is that u- negates a verb, while uma negates the existence of a noun phrase. A sentence like uma yutwan is ungrammatical; one says uyutwan "I am not going".

6.8 Existentials and possession

The verb tʼa means "be, exist". It is used for existence, location, and possession.

  • Tʼa-i-Ø kāla. → Tʼay kāla. "There is a house."
  • Tʼa-i-Ø kāla kālyami. → Tʼay kāla kālyami. "There is a house in the village."
  • Tʼa-i-Ø nakha kāla. → Tʼay nakha kāla. "I have a house." (literally "there is a house to me")

The possessed noun is absolutive; the possessor is dative. Negation with uma gives "have no":

  • Uma nakha kāla. "I have no house."

Nominal predicates need no copula:

  • Šiqʼa sa. "He is a person."
  • Šiqʼa sa ma? "Is he a person?"

Location is expressed either with the locative case or with the preposition a:

  • Tʼay tʰer kālami. "The man is in the house."
  • Yut-wa-Ø tʰer a gāla. "The man is going to the house."

7. Syntax

7.1 Clause structure and word order

The basic order is VSO: verb, subject, object. The language is strongly head-initial: nouns precede adjectives, genitives, and relative clauses; prepositions precede their objects; numerals and demonstratives precede the noun.

A transitive clause:

  • Xar-i-ši-Ø tʰeriš kāla. "The man saw the house."

An intransitive clause:

  • Wāy-i-Ø tʰer. "The man came."

An oblique argument follows the object:

  • Tʼāni-i-ši-Ø tʰeriš kāla nīsakha. "The man gave the house to the woman."

Word order is pragmatically flexible. The verb may be preceded by a fronted topic, and the subject may be postponed for emphasis, but VSO is the unmarked order and the one used in narrative.

7.2 Ergativity

Šiqʼāl is ergative–absolutive in both case marking and agreement. The single argument of an intransitive verb and the object of a transitive verb share the absolutive; the agent of a transitive verb takes the ergative.

ClauseASO
Wāyi tʰer. "The man came."ABS tʰer
Xariši tʰeriš kāla. "The man saw the house."ERG tʰerišABS kāla
Xariši tʰer. "He saw the man."ABS tʰer

The verb agrees accordingly: wāyi has only an absolutive suffix (zero), while xariši has an ergative suffix -ši and a zero absolutive.

Ergativity is consistent across aspects and moods; there is no split conditioned by tense or aspect. The antipassive is the main exception: it promotes the agent to absolutive and demotes the object to dative, producing an accusative-like pattern in that construction.

7.3 Questions

Polar questions are marked by the particle ma at the end of the clause:

  • Xar-i-ti-Ø taš sa ma? "Did you see him?"
  • Yut-wa-t ma? "Are you going?"

Content questions normally leave the interrogative word in situ, where the corresponding argument or adjunct would be:

  • Xar-i-ti-Ø taš kʰa? "Who did you see?"
  • Wāy-i-Ø kʰa? "Who came?"
  • Yut-wa-t kʰan? "Where are you going?"
  • Tʰana wāy-i-Ø? "When did he come?"
  • Kʰaru u-wāy-i-t? "Why didn't you come?"
  • Tʼar kʼur? "How many horses?"

The interrogative words are kʰa "who", kʰu "what", kʰan "where", tʰana "when", kʰaru "why", kʰar "how", tʼar "how many".

A questioned subject may be fronted for emphasis, especially in formal or literary style: Kʰa tʼay de yuti a qʼāla? "Who is it that goes to the sky?"

7.4 Relative clauses

Relative clauses follow the noun and are introduced by the particle de. The relativised argument is a gap; the verb retains its agreement with the gap, which is a useful cue to its role.

  • šiqʼa de wāy-i-Ø "the person who came"
  • kāla de kʼal-i-ni-Ø "the house that I built"
  • tʰer de kʼal-i-ši-Ø kāla "the man who built the house"
  • kʼur de xar-i-ni-Ø "the horse that I saw"

When the relativised argument is the agent of a transitive verb, the antipassive is common, especially if the object is indefinite:

  • tʰer de xar-ma-i-Ø "the man who was looking"

With a definite object, the plain verb is usual, as in tʰer de kʼal-i-ši-Ø kāla above.

Relative clauses are not marked for definiteness; šiqʼa de wāyi can mean "a person who came" or "the person who came" according to context.

7.5 Complement clauses and converbs

Complement clauses are introduced by de, the same particle as relative clauses. The complement clause is the object of the matrix verb.

  • Cʼar-i-ni-Ø de wāy-i-Ø sa. "I know that he came."
  • Xar-wa-i-ni-Ø de kʼal-wa-i-ši-Ø pātaš kāla. "I see that the master is building a house."

The verb of the complement clause is fully inflected. There is no infinitive; "want to go" is expressed with the desiderative suffix, not with a complement clause:

  • Yut-ul-wa-n. "I want to go." (not pʼal-wa-n de yut-wa-n)

Converbs. The suffix -te on a bare stem marks a same-subject medial clause: "having done X, ...". It carries no evidential or agreement.

  • Qʼan-te sa, pʼer-i-Ø pʼen. "Having heard this, the sheep fled."
  • Wāy-i-te, xar-i-ši-Ø tʰeriš kāla. "He came and saw the house."

7.6 Coordination

Noun phrases are coordinated with the enclitic -ya "and", attached to the first conjunct, and xu "or" between the conjuncts:

  • Tʰer-ya nīsa "the man and the woman"
  • Tʰer xu nīsa "the man or the woman"

Clauses are simply juxtaposed, or linked with ya:

  • Wāy-i-Ø tʰer, ya xar-i-ši-Ø kāla. "The man came, and he saw the house."

The particle xu can also coordinate clauses: Yut-wa-n xu sūl-wa-n? "Shall I go or stay?"

7.7 Information structure

The unmarked order is VSO, but the clause-initial position is a topic position. A fronted constituent is interpreted as topic or contrastive focus:

  • Kāla, xar-i-ši-Ø tʰeriš. "The house — the man saw it."
  • Na, u-yut-wa-n. "Me, I'm not going."

New information tends to stay in situ, towards the end of the clause. The particle de can also mark a focused complement: De sa xar-i-ni-Ø. "It's this that I saw." This is a literary construction and rare in speech.

Noun–noun compounds are head-final: the second element is the head.

  • tʰawa "sun" + tʼāla "light" → tʰawatʼāla "sunlight"

Verb–noun compounds are also common: xūl "heart" + qʼen "die" → xūlqʼen "forget" (literally "the heart dies").

  • kʼalmi — building site, workshop
  • kʼalca — tool
  • kʼaltʼa — a building, the thing built
  • kʼaltʼi — cause to build, have built
  • kʼalkʰu — be able to build
  • kʼalul — want to build

  • šiqʼ — speak
  • šiqʼa — person, speaker
  • šiqʼi — speaker, orator
  • šiqʼāl — speech, language
  • šiqʼmi — place of speaking, assembly ground
